\section{Introduction}

We start with an informal introduction to quantum computing and lay some motivations.

Fault-tolerant quantum computing has the potential, through algorithms like Shor's factoring algorithm \cite{Shor}, to deliver possibly exponential speedups over classical computation. However, current Noisy Intermediate-Scale Quantum (NISQ) quantum computers have too few qubits and too much noise to deliver significant practical benefit \cite{PreskillNISQ}.

Computation in the NISQ era is often modelled by a hybrid setup: on the quantum computer, a noisy initial state is prepared, some noisy gates are applied, and qubits are measured. A classical computer then processes these measurements, and can modify and repeat the quantum computation if necessary.

For the quantum computer, there exist a variety of computational models: The quantum Turing machine \cite{QuantumTM}, the quantum circuit model \cite{QuantumCircuit}, adiabatic quantum computation \cite{Adiabatic}, measurement-based models \cite{MBQCOriginal}, among many others. Though these models are equivalent from a complexity viewpoint (informally, any problem that is efficiently solvable in one is efficiently solvable in the others) \cite{MBQCOriginal,AdiabaticEqCircuit,CircuitEqTM}, the underlying quantum properties used by each may differ significantly.

The measurement-based quantum computation (MBQC) model is of particular interest. This is because MBQC collapses any quantum circuit into measurements of a highly-entangled resource state that can be prepared in constant depth. This is quite shocking, as no clear classical counterpart exists. The cost of this model, however, is that measurements need to be adaptive. In other words, some post-processing must be done on-the-fly to determine what qubits should be measured next.

Since measurement is irreversible, MBQC leads to a model of one-way quantum computation. In other words, as measurement cannot increase the entanglement of a system, all entanglement required must be initially present in the resource state. Informally, entanglement is the resource consumed by this model of quantum computation. Thus, there is significant interest in determining what resource states are universal for quantum computation. That is, what resource states, under the MBQC model, can generate any possible arrangement of qubits?

The comparability grid is the graph with vertices $V = \{(x,y) \mid x,y \in [n]\}$ and an edge from $(x_1,y_1)$ to $(x_2,y_2)$ if $x_1 \geq x_2$ and $y_1 \geq y_2$. It is an open problem whether the family of all comparability grids is universal for MBQC. There are powerful theoretical implications for both possibilities. For example, if this family is universal, then any universal family of graph states must have unbounded rank width [cite:KunalMD]. If comparability grids are not universal, then unbounded rank width is not a sufficient condition, since the family of all comparability grids have unbounded rank width.

In this thesis, we explore the comparability grid's potential as a resource state for universal quantum computation. In section 2, we introduce the preliminaries necessary for such an endeavour, before exploring various algorithmic and theoretical approaches in later sections. We conclude with a discussion of similar results pertaining to circle graphs.

\subsection{Informal Summary of Results}

We informally summarize the main results of this thesis. Our overall goal is to determine the universality of the comparability grids for universal MBQC. This paper will not provide a definitive result to this open problem. Instead, we will lay out a series of algorithmic and theoretical approaches to more finely characterize whether the comparability grid is a universal resource.

From the algorithmic perspective, we give a $O(n\log n)$ algorithm to determine whether a subset of the comparability grid is ``grid-like,'' that is, has a few edges in addition to the regular grid. Then, the tabelau formalism for Clifford circuits and a linear-algebra-based universal enumeration algorithm is introduced to check whether this ``grid-like'' structure has the potential for universal quantum computation. This pipeline forms the backbone of our results.

From the theoretical perspective, we recite known results regarding local unitary and local clifford equivalence; vertex-minors; local complementation. We recall some known results on the infeasibility of an enumeration or algorithmic proof based on these mechanisms. In particular, determining vertex minors is $\NP$-hard [cite:need].  

We use the theoretical recitations to provide a series of conjectures on the universality of comparability grids in the conclusion section.
