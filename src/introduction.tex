\section{Introduction}

We start with an informal introduction to quantum computing and lay some motivations.

Fault-tolerant quantum computing has the potential, through algorithms like Shor's factoring algorithm [cite:shor], to deliver possibly exponential speedups over classical computation. However, current Noisy Intermediate-Scale Quantum (NISQ) quantum computers have too few qubits and too much noise to deliver significant practical benefit [cite:preskill2018].

Computation in the NISQ era is often modelled by a hybrid setup: on the quantum computer, a noisy initial state is prepared, some noisy gates are applied, and qubits are measured. A classical computer then processes these measurements, and can modify and repeat the quantum computation if necessary.

For the quantum computer, there exist a variety of computational models: The quantum Turing machine [cite:need], the quantum circuit model [cite:need], adiabatic quantum computation [cite:need], measurement-based models [cite:need], among many others. Though these models are equivalent from a complexity viewpoint (informally, any problem that is efficiently solvable in one is efficiently solvable in the others) [cite:need], the underlying quantum properties used and their implementation may differ significantly.

The measurement-based quantum computation (MBQC) model is of particular interest. This is because MBQC collapses any quantum circuit into measurements of a highly-entangled resource state that can be prepared in constant depth. This is quite shocking, as no clear classical counterpart exists. The cost of this model, however, is that measurements need to be adaptive. In other words, some post-processing must be done on-the-fly to determine what qubits should be measured next.

Since measurement is irreversible, MBQC leads to a model of one-way quantum computation. In other words, as measurement cannot increase the entanglement of a system, all entanglement required must be initially present in the resource state. Informally, entanglement is the resource consumed by this model of quantum computation. Thus, there is significant interest in determining what resource states are universal for quantum computation. That is, what resource states that, under the MBQC model, can generate any possible arrangement of qubits. 

In this thesis, we explore the comparability grid's potential as a resource state for universal quantum computation. In section 2, we introduce the preliminaries necessary for such an endeavour, before exploring various algorithmic and theoretical approaches in later sections.
